1.  FINANCIAL LITERACY: THE STARTING POINT

Financial literacy is more than knowing the names of bank accounts or
investment products. The FAME publication defines financial literacy as
a combination of financial awareness, knowledge, skills, attitude and
behaviour necessary to make sound financial decisions and ultimately
achieve individual financial well-being. It therefore has three closely
connected dimensions: financial knowledge, financial behaviour and
financial attitude.

Financial knowledge means understanding important concepts and being
able to evaluate them in real financial situations. Examples include
simple interest, compound interest, time value of money, inflation,
diversification, risk-return relationships and interest paid on loans.
Knowledge by itself, however, does not guarantee good outcomes. A person
may understand compounding and still fail to save regularly.

Financial behaviour concerns what a person actually does with money: how
income and expenses are managed, whether savings are made, whether
investments are planned, whether credit is used responsibly and whether
a safety net is built. Financial attitude concerns the way a person
responds to saving, spending, risk and long-term security. A disciplined
financial system therefore combines understanding with repeatable
behaviour.

The second source similarly emphasizes that financial education should
help individuals understand and plan income and expenses, build and
manage assets and liabilities, develop budgeting, saving and investing
habits, and secure the future through insurance and pension. A useful
principle emerging from both publications is simple: money management
should be deliberate rather than reactive.

A strong financial system begins with four questions: - What money comes
in? - What money goes out? - What financial obligations already exist? -
What future goals must the money support?

The answers create the foundation for every later decision.

2.  DETERMINE YOUR FINANCIAL POSITION

Before selecting a financial product, establish your current financial
position. The securities-market booklet presents this as an initial
step: assess income, expenses, assets and liabilities, calculate net
worth, set financial goals and prepare a SMART plan.

Start by listing all income sources. Depending on circumstances, income
may include salary, business income, rent, interest, pension and other
legitimate receipts. Then list all expenditure. Separate essential
expenses such as food, housing, education, transport, healthcare and
utilities from discretionary expenses such as shopping, dining out and
entertainment.

Next, list assets. Examples include bank balances, fixed deposits,
investments, property and other financial or physical assets. Then list
liabilities such as outstanding loans, credit-card dues and other
repayment obligations.

A basic net-worth calculation is:

NET WORTH = TOTAL ASSETS - TOTAL LIABILITIES

Net worth is not a complete measure of financial health, but it is a
useful starting point. A person with high assets but excessive
short-term debt may still have financial stress. Similarly, a young
person with limited assets but controlled expenses, regular savings and
manageable liabilities may be building a strong position.

The important point is to make the financial position visible. Once
income, spending, assets and liabilities are written down, financial
decisions become easier to evaluate.

3.  INCOME MANAGEMENT

Income is the foundation of personal finance because savings and
investment capacity ultimately come from available income after
necessary expenditure. The securities-market booklet describes income as
money earned by an individual or household from sources such as salary,
business, rent, interest, pension and government schemes.

Income management starts by understanding amount, frequency and source.
A salaried employee may receive income monthly; a business owner may
experience variable cash flows; a person receiving rent or interest may
have periodic receipts. The budget should reflect actual timing, not
merely annual totals.

Do not confuse gross income with money actually available for spending.
Financial planning should account for taxes, mandatory deductions and
unavoidable commitments where applicable. Once usable income is known,
allocate it deliberately.

The source booklet also encourages diversification of income sources
over time, for example by developing skills, taking part-time work or
investing in income-generating assets. This should not be interpreted as
a requirement to pursue every possible income stream. The useful lesson
is that dependence on a single source can make a financial plan more
vulnerable to interruption.

Income should first support necessities and financial obligations, then
planned savings and investments, and finally discretionary consumption.
The precise proportions will vary by household and goal.

4.  EXPENDITURE MANAGEMENT

Expenditure is money spent to meet daily needs and financial
obligations. The securities-market booklet divides expenditure
conceptually into essentials and discretionary spending. Essentials
include items such as food, housing, education, transport, healthcare
and utility bills. Discretionary expenditure includes shopping, dining
out and entertainment.

The first rule is visibility. Track spending rather than relying on
memory. The source recommends keeping a daily record of income and
expenses for at least six months. This creates a realistic picture of
recurring expenses, irregular expenses and spending leakage.

The second rule is classification. A purchase should be evaluated as a
need, a want or an avoidable expense. This is not a moral judgment. It
is a budgeting tool. A discretionary expense can be perfectly reasonable
when it fits within a plan; the problem arises when discretionary
spending consumes money required for obligations, savings or emergency
reserves.

The third rule is review. Compare planned spending with actual spending.
If a category repeatedly exceeds its budget, investigate the cause. The
answer may be an unrealistic budget, a forgotten annual expense,
lifestyle inflation or unnecessary consumption.

A practical expenditure-control sequence is: 1. Record. 2. Categorize.
3. Set a limit. 4. Compare actual spending with the limit. 5. Correct
the next period’s allocation.

The FAME publication similarly emphasizes that a budget helps control
expenses and that comparing budgeted expenditure with actual expenditure
shows where spending was higher or lower than planned.

5.  BUDGETING

A budget is a plan for future income and expenses. It can be weekly,
monthly or yearly. Its purpose is not merely to restrict spending; it is
to allocate money so that current needs, future goals and financial
safety can coexist.

The source publications emphasize several benefits of budgeting:
controlling unnecessary expenses, tracking income and spending, saving
for short- and long-term goals, reducing financial stress and preventing
debt accumulation.

A practical three-step budgeting process is: Step 1: Know your income
sources. Step 2: List your essential and non-essential expenses. Step 3:
Set spending limits and save before spending.

The securities-market booklet presents the 50-30-20 principle as a
useful starting framework: approximately 50% for needs, 30% for wants
and 20% for savings, while explicitly noting that the proportions may
vary according to the goal. Treat this as a framework, not an inflexible
law.

The FAME approach gives the stronger behavioural message: saving should
be treated as a planned part of income rather than whatever remains
accidentally at the end of the month.

A good budget should also account for irregular costs. Annual insurance
premiums, education payments, repairs, festivals, travel and other
non-monthly expenses should not be allowed to appear as “unexpected”
merely because they occur infrequently.

Review the budget monthly. If income changes, debt changes or a major
goal changes, revise the allocation.

6.  SAVING AND THE SAVE-FIRST MINDSET

Saving is the portion of income that is not spent and is kept aside for
future use. The FAME publication expresses the relationship as:

SAVING = INCOME - EXPENDITURE

It also presents a behavioural alternative:

EXPENDITURE = INCOME - SAVING

The second formulation captures the save-first mindset. Instead of
spending first and hoping something remains, set aside a planned amount
before discretionary expenditure.

The securities-market booklet similarly presents a mindset shift:

Income - Expenses = Saving versus Income - Saving = Expenses

The practical rule is: “Save first. Spend what is left.”

Saving serves several purposes. It provides a cushion for emergencies,
supports short-term and long-term goals, reduces dependence on expensive
credit and creates capital that can later be invested.

The second source suggests saving at least 20% of monthly income as an
ideal target and maintaining an emergency fund equal to six months of
expenses. These are educational guidelines rather than universal
requirements; actual savings capacity depends on income, obligations,
household structure and financial goals.

Savings should be accessible when needed and should not all be locked
into products that are difficult to liquidate. The FAME publication
emphasizes keeping some savings in liquid assets and avoiding very risky
or unregulated instruments.

7.  SAVINGS PRODUCTS AND THE SAFETY-LIQUIDITY-RETURN FRAMEWORK

The FAME publication identifies three important considerations when
saving: Safety, Liquidity and Return.

Safety refers to how certain the preservation of principal and expected
return are. The source describes government bonds as among the safest
and bank fixed deposits as comparatively safe.

Liquidity refers to how easily an asset can be converted into cash with
minimum loss of value. Bank deposits, listed and traded equity shares
and mutual funds are described as comparatively liquid, although actual
liquidity and market value can differ across products.

Return refers to the income or gain generated by the financial product.
Higher-return possibilities can involve greater risk. An equity share
may generate higher returns but can also suffer a higher loss.

The securities-market booklet describes several savings-related
products. Savings accounts generally provide easy deposits and
withdrawals and may earn interest. Current accounts are primarily
intended for businesses, firms and traders and generally do not provide
interest on the balance. Fixed deposits involve placing a lump sum for a
fixed period at a fixed rate, with premature withdrawal generally
subject to a penalty. Recurring deposits involve regular deposits over a
fixed tenure.

The key principle is that no single product automatically maximizes
safety, liquidity and return simultaneously. A financial plan should
match each pool of money to its purpose.

For example, money required for an immediate emergency should prioritize
accessibility and stability. Money intended for a distant goal can
potentially accept more investment risk if appropriate.

8.  FINANCIAL GOALS AND THE SMART FRAMEWORK

A financial goal is a specific target to achieve with money within a
defined time frame. Goals give savings and investment decisions a
purpose.

The securities-market booklet divides examples into: - Short-term goals:
approximately 0-3 years, such as an emergency fund, vacation or buying a
gadget. - Medium-term goals: approximately 3-7 years, such as buying a
car, higher education or upgrading home appliances. - Long-term goals:
7+ years, such as buying a house, planning for a child’s marriage or
retirement.

Goals should follow the SMART framework: S - Specific M - Measurable A -
Attainable R - Relevant T - Time bound

For example, “I want a scooter” is vague. A SMART goal would specify the
target amount, monthly saving requirement, purpose and deadline. The
source gives an illustration of saving Rs. 7,500 per month for 12 months
toward a Rs. 90,000 scooter needed for daily commuting.

The goal should then determine the financial product, not the other way
around. If the goal is near-term, a highly volatile asset may be
unsuitable because the money may be needed during a market decline. A
long-term goal can potentially tolerate more volatility if the investor
has the appropriate risk capacity and discipline.

Every major financial goal should therefore contain five fields: target
amount, current amount, deadline, required contribution and chosen
financial vehicle.

9.  INFLATION AND PURCHASING POWER

Inflation is the rate of increase in prices of goods and services over
time. When prices rise, the purchasing power of money falls.

Suppose a product costs Rs. 100 today and Rs. 110 a year later. The
increase is Rs. 10, equivalent to 10% inflation over that period for
that example.

Inflation matters because a nominally positive return can still
represent a loss in purchasing power. FAME provides an illustrative
comparison: if an investment earns 6% while inflation is 4%, the simple
real-return illustration is approximately 2%. If money earns 0% while
inflation is 4%, the illustrative real return is -4%.

The securities-market booklet gives a similar example: if inflation is
6% while a savings account gives 4%, the money may lose purchasing power
in real terms even though the account balance increases numerically.

Inflation must therefore be incorporated into long-term goals. A
retirement target calculated only using today’s prices can be inadequate
if future living costs are substantially higher.

The source encourages long-term investing and notes that assets such as
mutual funds, gold, property or stocks may have the potential to grow
faster than inflation, although these investments carry different risks
and are not guaranteed to outperform inflation over every period.

10. TIME VALUE OF MONEY

The time value of money (TVM) means that money available now can be
worth more than the same nominal amount received in the future because
current money can potentially earn a return.

FAME illustrates the concept using a 10% annual compound rate. Rs. 100
invested for two years becomes Rs. 121. Therefore, Rs. 121 received two
years later has a present value of Rs. 100 under that assumed rate.

Two concepts follow: Future Value (FV): what current money can grow
into. Present Value (PV): what a future amount is worth today at a
chosen discount rate.

TVM matters when comparing investment alternatives, loans, future
expenses and long-term goals. It also explains why starting early can be
powerful: money invested earlier has more time to compound.

TVM should not be interpreted as a guarantee of investment return. The
calculation depends on the assumed rate, compounding frequency and time
period. Actual market investments can have uncertain returns.

A practical guideline is to evaluate large financial commitments in
terms of timing as well as amount. Receiving or paying Rs. 100 today is
not economically identical to receiving or paying Rs. 100 years later.

11. COMPOUNDING

Compounding is often described as “interest on interest.” The return
earned is added to the principal and subsequently earns further returns.

FAME gives a simple illustration: Rs. 10,000 at 10% compounded annually
becomes Rs. 11,000 after year one, Rs. 12,100 after year two and Rs.
13,310 after year three.

The general formula is:

A = P x (1 + r/n)^(n x t)

where: A = amount, P = principal, r = annual interest rate expressed as
a decimal, n = number of compounding periods per year, t = time in
years.

The source also illustrates the effect over ten years: Rs. 10,000
earning 10% simple interest would become Rs. 20,000, while 10%
compounded quarterly becomes approximately Rs. 26,851 under the stated
assumptions.

The important lesson is not to chase a particular rate but to understand
time and reinvestment. Long holding periods can magnify the effect of
compounding.

Compounding works in both directions. Investments can compound
positively when returns are reinvested, while borrowing can become
expensive when interest and charges accumulate. This is why the cost of
debt must be understood before borrowing.

12. INTEREST ON LOANS AND THE TRUE COST OF BORROWING

Interest is the cost of borrowing money. FAME provides a simple-interest
illustration: borrowing Rs. 100 for one year at 10% simple interest
produces Rs. 10 interest and Rs. 110 total repayment under that
simplified example.

The publication warns that the rate printed in a prominent location may
not communicate the complete cost. Borrowers should read the loan
sanction letter, terms and conditions and fine print. It specifically
warns that a charge described as 2% per month would amount to 24% per
year if annualized in that simple manner.

The source also states that banks do not offer simple-interest-rate
loans as represented by the educational example; the example is designed
to explain the mathematics.

Before borrowing, determine: - Principal borrowed. - Interest rate and
how it is calculated. - Annualized cost where applicable. - Processing
fees. - Penalties and other charges. - Repayment frequency. - Tenure. -
Total repayment obligation. - Conditions for early repayment or
foreclosure. - Consequences of delayed payment.

Do not judge a loan solely by the monthly EMI. A lower EMI can result
from a longer tenure and therefore a higher total cost.

For digital loans, the source specifically emphasizes checking the
Annual Percentage Rate and the Key Fact Statement before accepting the
loan.

13. RESPONSIBLE BORROWING AND CREDIT SCORES

Borrowing is appropriate when it supports a manageable financial purpose
and the repayment burden fits the borrower’s means. FAME gives examples
of borrowing that creates value or supports long-term benefit, such as a
mortgage for a house or an education loan for higher education. It
contrasts this with irresponsible use of credit for discretionary
personal expenditure.

Borrow from regulated entities such as banks, NBFCs and HFCs where
possible. The source explains that regulated entities are supervised and
have grievance-redress mechanisms. Borrowers should be cautious of
agents who charge fees merely to “facilitate” a loan and should deal
directly with the bank, NBFC, HFC or authorized Business Correspondent.

A credit score is a three-digit indicator of creditworthiness based on
credit history and other factors. The FAME publication explains that
timely repayment generally supports a higher score. Financial
institutions may consider the score and credit history, along with other
factors, when evaluating a loan. A higher score may, all else equal,
help a borrower access credit at a lower rate.

Good credit behaviour includes: - Borrow only what you can repay
regularly and on time. - Never deliberately miss instalments. - Monitor
repayment dates. - Review your credit report. - Correct inaccurate
information. - Repay earlier where financially appropriate and where
doing so supports a good credit history.

The source states that individuals have access to one free full credit
report, including credit score, once each calendar year, with access
procedures available from the respective credit information companies.

14. EMERGENCY FUNDS

An emergency fund is a financial safety net intended for unexpected
expenses and income disruptions. The securities-market booklet presents
six months of expenses as an emergency-fund target.

An emergency fund should be distinguished from long-term investment
capital. Its primary objective is resilience, not maximum return. The
money should therefore be accessible and relatively stable.

A practical emergency-fund calculation is:

EMERGENCY FUND TARGET = MONTHLY ESSENTIAL EXPENSES x TARGET NUMBER OF
MONTHS

If essential expenses are Rs. 30,000 per month and the chosen target is
six months, the illustrative target is Rs. 1,80,000.

The correct target varies with employment stability, dependents,
insurance coverage, debt, household structure and other factors. The
important source-based principle is to build a safety reserve before
relying heavily on long-term market investments.

An emergency fund reduces the probability that a sudden expense will
force the household to sell investments at an unfavorable time or borrow
at a high cost.

15. INSURANCE AND PENSION PLANNING

Insurance is a financial protection tool. It is designed to transfer
specified financial risks to an insurer in exchange for a premium under
the terms of the policy. The securities-market booklet identifies
insurance as a component of securing one’s future along with pension
planning.

Insurance and investment should not automatically be combined. FAME’s
mis-selling guidance specifically recommends assessing insurance needs
and investment requirements separately and taking an insurance policy or
investment product only when it is actually needed.

Before buying insurance, understand the purpose of the policy, the
coverage, exclusions, premium, duration and important conditions. Never
sign blank forms and do not accept a product merely because someone
describes it as a mandatory add-on to another financial service unless
the requirement is legitimate and clearly documented.

Pension planning addresses the need for income after working years. The
securities-market booklet describes the National Pension System (NPS),
regulated by PFRDA, as a government-backed retirement scheme in which
individuals can invest during working years and receive pension and
lump-sum benefits after retirement, subject to the applicable rules. The
money can be invested in equity, corporate bonds and government
securities depending on the investor’s age and choice, and the booklet
notes tax benefits and partial withdrawal provisions.

Retirement planning should begin early because the horizon is long and
compounding can become significant. The correct approach is to define
the desired retirement lifestyle, estimate future expenses, account for
inflation, identify existing retirement assets and determine a
disciplined contribution strategy.

16. SAVING VERSUS INVESTING

Saving and investing are related but not identical. Saving means setting
aside income for future use. Investment means putting saved money into
financial instruments or assets with the objective of generating returns
over time.

The securities-market booklet states that investments involve some
degree of risk but offer potential for higher growth. Examples include
mutual funds, fixed deposits, shares, bonds, gold and real estate.

Savings generally emphasize safety and accessibility. Investments
introduce a wider range of risk-return outcomes. Therefore, the money
needed for immediate obligations should not automatically be placed into
volatile assets merely because those assets have historically produced
higher long-term returns.

Before investing, determine: 1. Financial goal. 2. Time horizon. 3. Risk
appetite and risk capacity. 4. Required liquidity. 5. Appropriate asset
allocation. 6. Product costs and tax implications. 7. Regulatory status
of the provider.

The source’s investment message can be summarized as: invest early,
invest regularly and invest wisely.

17. RISK AND RETURN

Every investment entails some degree of risk. FAME describes a general
relationship: higher expected return generally comes with higher risk,
including the possibility of losing part or all of the initial
investment. Lower expected returns are generally associated with lower
risk.

Risk is not one-dimensional. It can include market-price volatility,
credit or default risk, liquidity risk, interest-rate risk,
concentration risk, inflation risk and operational or fraud risk.

The securities-market booklet emphasizes matching the product to the
goal, time frame and risk appetite. A high-return product is not
automatically better. A product that is inappropriate for the investor’s
time horizon can be harmful even if its long-term return potential is
attractive.

The booklet uses the idea that short-term fluctuations can be normal in
market investments and encourages long-term thinking. Its key message is
that volatility and risk are not identical concepts. Volatility
describes fluctuations in price; risk also includes the possibility that
the investment will not meet the investor’s objective or that capital
may be permanently impaired.

A disciplined investor therefore asks: - What can I lose? - Under what
circumstances can I lose it? - How long can the loss last? - Can I
tolerate that loss without abandoning the plan? - Does the expected
return justify the risk?

18. ASSET ALLOCATION

Asset allocation is the strategy of distributing money across different
asset classes. The securities-market booklet identifies asset allocation
and diversification as core financial concepts.

Typical asset categories mentioned in the two publications include bank
deposits, bonds, equity shares, mutual funds, gold and real estate. Each
behaves differently.

The correct allocation depends on goal, time horizon and risk capacity.
A short-term emergency reserve should not be treated the same as a
retirement portfolio. A person saving for a near-term purchase may need
higher stability and liquidity, whereas a long-term investor may be able
to tolerate more equity-market volatility.

Asset allocation should also reflect the investor’s overall financial
position. A person whose employment, business and property exposure are
all concentrated in one economic sector may need to think carefully
before concentrating investments in the same area.

Do not choose an allocation simply because it is fashionable. The
appropriate allocation is the one that can be maintained through
different market conditions while remaining aligned with the goal.

19. DIVERSIFICATION

Diversification means spreading investments across different assets
rather than putting all money into one asset. FAME uses the familiar
idea of not putting all eggs in one basket.

Diversification reduces concentration risk. If one investment performs
poorly, the entire financial position is less likely to be affected to
the same degree when other investments behave differently.

Diversification can occur across: - Asset classes. - Companies. -
Sectors. - Investment products. - Time periods. - Geographical exposure
where appropriate.

However, diversification does not eliminate risk. A portfolio containing
many securities can still fall in value if the overall market declines.
Diversification is a risk-management technique, not a guarantee of
profit.

The FAME publication specifically advises that savings be invested in
diversified instruments and that some portion be kept in liquid assets.
It also warns against placing money in highly risky or unregulated
instruments.

The securities-market booklet similarly advises against putting all
money into a single stock or sector. The objective is to construct a
portfolio whose risks are understandable and manageable.

20. INVESTMENT OPTIONS

The securities-market booklet discusses a broad range of investment
options.

Bank Fixed Deposits: A fixed amount is deposited for a specified period
at a pre-decided interest rate. The booklet describes FDs as traditional
and comparatively safe. It states that, under the DICGC rules described
in the booklet, bank deposits are insured up to Rs. 5 lakh per depositor
per bank in the circumstances covered by deposit insurance.

Public Provident Fund (PPF): The booklet describes PPF as a
government-backed savings scheme with a 15-year lock-in, tax-free
interest as described in the source, annual contribution limits of Rs.
500 minimum and Rs. 1.5 lakh maximum, and partial withdrawal provisions
after five financial years subject to the rules.

Mutual Funds: Asset Management Companies pool investors’ money and
invest in diversified portfolios of stocks, bonds or hybrid assets.
Professional fund managers manage the portfolios.

Stocks or Equity Shares: Buying shares means owning a part of a publicly
listed company. Returns can arise through capital gains and dividends,
but stock prices can fluctuate substantially.

Real Estate: Property can generate rental income and capital
appreciation but generally requires substantial capital and is less
liquid. Maintenance costs, taxes and market fluctuations should be
considered.

Gold: The booklet describes physical gold and Gold ETFs and presents
gold as an asset that can diversify a portfolio. Gold prices can
fluctuate in the short term.

National Pension System: NPS is described as a retirement-focused
government-backed scheme regulated by PFRDA.

Post Office Schemes: The booklet mentions schemes such as Monthly Income
Scheme, Senior Citizen Savings Scheme, National Savings Scheme and Kisan
Vikas Patra, describing them as government-backed options intended
particularly for savers.

Bonds and Debentures: These are debt instruments in which money is lent
to a government or company in exchange for interest and repayment
according to the terms. Government bonds are described as safer than
corporate bonds, while corporate bonds may offer higher returns with
somewhat higher risk.

21. SECURITIES MARKETS AND THEIR ECOSYSTEM

The securities market is a platform where instruments such as shares,
bonds, debentures, mutual-fund units and derivatives are bought and
sold. It connects investors who have capital with entities such as
companies and governments that need funds.

The securities-market booklet distinguishes: Primary Market: securities
are issued for the first time, such as when a company conducts an IPO.
Secondary Market: securities already issued and listed are bought and
sold among market participants.

The ecosystem contains several participants: - SEBI: regulator and
investor-protection authority for the securities market. - Stock
exchanges such as NSE and BSE: platforms for securities trading. -
Depositories such as NSDL and CDSL: hold securities in dematerialized
form. - Brokers: SEBI-registered intermediaries facilitating
transactions. - Asset Management Companies: manage mutual funds. -
Investment Advisers: SEBI-registered professionals providing investment
advice within the applicable framework. - Banks: provide payment,
transfer and ASBA-related services. - Rating agencies: provide ratings
for certain debt instruments. - Registrars and Transfer Agents: maintain
records and process relevant investor services.

The source summarizes a practical investor mindset with the word SAFETY:
S - use SEBI-registered platforms. A - avoid tips and traps. F - focus
on the long term. E - educate yourself. T - track performance and taxes.
Y - remember that your goals matter most.

22. SEBI AND INVESTOR PROTECTION

SEBI is the Securities and Exchange Board of India. The
securities-market booklet describes SEBI as the regulator of the
securities market and protector of investors’ interests, with
responsibilities that include regulation and promotion of orderly market
development.

The practical implication for an investor is verification. Before
handing money or personal information to a market intermediary, confirm
that the entity is appropriately registered and that the service being
offered matches the entity’s regulatory status.

Investor protection also depends on investor behaviour. Regulation
cannot substitute for reading documents, checking transactions,
preserving records and reporting suspicious activity.

The booklet repeatedly warns against unregistered advisers, fake
investment platforms, guaranteed-return promises, social-media tips and
pressure tactics.

A useful rule is to separate three questions: 1. Is the provider
legitimate and regulated? 2. Is the product itself suitable? 3. Are the
terms, risks, costs and taxes understood?

A “yes” to only the first question is insufficient. A regulated
intermediary can still offer a product that is inappropriate for a
particular goal, and a legitimate product can still be unsuitable if the
investor does not understand its risk.

23. PREPARING TO INVEST IN SECURITIES MARKETS

The securities-market booklet presents a basic preparation sequence: 1.
Open or link a bank account. 2. Complete KYC. 3. Open a Demat and
Trading Account with a SEBI-registered stock broker or depository
participant. 4. Begin with understandable investments and proceed
according to the investor’s goals and risk capacity.

KYC is a mandatory identity and address verification process. The
booklet lists documents such as PAN, Aadhaar linked to a mobile number,
photograph, signature and bank proof, subject to the applicable
onboarding process.

A Demat account holds securities in dematerialized form. A trading
account provides the mechanism to buy and sell securities through a
broker.

For IPO investing, the booklet describes ASBA, under which an investor
authorizes a Self-Certified Syndicate Bank to block funds in the
relevant account rather than transferring the application money away
immediately. The source notes that ASBA can be used for specified public
and rights issues under the applicable framework.

Before investing, ensure that account credentials are secure, contact
details are updated and statements can be reviewed regularly.

24. EQUITY AND SHARES

A share represents ownership in a company. Equity investors can
potentially earn through price appreciation and dividends.

Equity carries market risk. Share prices can change because of company
performance, economic conditions, industry developments, investor
expectations and many other factors.

The securities-market booklet contrasts an old belief that “stock market
is risky” with a more informed perspective: lack of knowledge can itself
be risky, while markets can provide opportunities when approached with
education and discipline. It also emphasizes that volatility is part of
market investing.

Do not interpret this as a claim that equity is always safe in the long
term. Long-term holding can help an investor ride through temporary
fluctuations, but a weak company, excessive valuation, concentration or
poor investment thesis can still cause significant loss.

Before buying an individual stock, understand the company, its business
model, financial performance, industry, risks and valuation
considerations. Do not rely solely on social-media tips, WhatsApp
messages or promotional claims.

Diversification is especially important for investors who hold
individual shares. A single-company investment can expose the portfolio
to company-specific risk that cannot be removed by simply waiting.

25. BONDS AND DEBENTURES

Bonds and debentures are debt instruments. The investor lends money to a
government or company in exchange for interest and repayment according
to the terms.

The securities-market booklet describes them as instruments that can
provide regular and relatively predictable income. Government bonds are
described as safer than corporate bonds, while corporate bonds may
provide higher returns with somewhat higher risk.

Bond investing still requires analysis. “Fixed interest” does not mean
“zero risk.” Relevant considerations include issuer quality, maturity,
interest rate, liquidity and the terms governing repayment.

Credit risk is especially important for corporate debt: the issuer must
be able to meet interest and principal obligations. Market values can
also change before maturity because interest rates and market conditions
change.

Before investing in a bond or debenture, read the documentation and
understand: - Issuer. - Coupon or interest terms. - Maturity. -
Repayment structure. - Credit quality or rating where relevant. -
Liquidity. - Applicable costs and taxes. - Conditions under which the
security can be sold.

The central guideline is to understand what you are lending to, for how
long and under what repayment terms.

26. MUTUAL FUNDS AND ETFs

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a
portfolio managed according to a stated scheme objective. The
securities-market booklet describes mutual funds as professionally
managed and capable of providing diversification and easy entry.

Mutual-fund risk varies by scheme. Equity funds generally carry greater
market risk than many debt-oriented funds, while hybrid funds combine
asset classes.

An ETF, or Exchange Traded Fund, is a basket of assets that trades like
a security on an exchange. The source lists ETFs among securities-market
products and discusses index-linked investing.

Key mutual-fund terms: NAV: Net Asset Value, the per-unit value of a
mutual-fund scheme. AMC: Asset Management Company managing the mutual
fund. Fund Manager: the professional responsible for investment
decisions. Expense Ratio: a fee associated with managing the fund.

The Riskometer is presented as a tool that helps investors understand a
mutual-fund scheme’s risk level, ranging from Low to Very High.
Investors should select funds based on risk capacity and financial goals
rather than return advertisements alone.

The booklet also emphasizes that mutual funds are market-linked and that
returns are not fixed like an FD.

27. MUTUAL-FUND SELECTION, DIRECT/REGULAR PLANS AND SIPS

Mutual-fund selection should begin with the goal, not with the highest
recent return.

The source distinguishes Direct Plans and Regular Plans. A Direct Plan
involves investing directly with the AMC without a distributor, broker
or intermediary and generally has a lower expense ratio because
distribution commissions are not included in the same manner. The
investor takes responsibility for making decisions.

A Regular Plan is purchased through a distributor, adviser or broker,
with intermediary remuneration reflected in the structure. The booklet
presents professional guidance as one potential advantage for new or
less-informed investors.

Investors should understand the difference and the associated cost
before choosing.

SIP stands for Systematic Investment Plan. It is a method of investing a
predetermined amount at predetermined intervals in a mutual-fund scheme.
The source notes that SIP frequency can be daily, weekly, fortnightly,
monthly or quarterly depending on the scheme and setup.

SIP is a method of investing, not a guarantee of profit. Its value comes
from disciplined periodic investing and, in market-linked assets, from
avoiding the need to predict a single perfect entry point.

The source also explains that a missed SIP payment does not necessarily
mean the investment itself must be cancelled. It describes pausing and
restarting a SIP as an available approach, subject to the applicable
scheme and platform terms.

The booklet introduces “Choti SIP” as a small-ticket SIP initiative
proposed by SEBI, with the source describing a starting amount as low as
Rs. 250 per month.

28. REITs, INVITs AND COMMODITY DERIVATIVES

REITs and InvITs provide a way to invest in real-estate and
infrastructure assets through listed units. The securities-market
booklet describes them as investments that can provide regular income
and potential capital appreciation, with moderate risk as presented in
the source.

The key attraction is exposure to underlying assets without directly
purchasing an entire property or infrastructure project. However, their
market prices can fluctuate, and the investor should understand the
structure, underlying assets, distributions, fees and risks.

Commodity derivatives are contracts linked to commodities such as gold,
silver, oil or wheat. Their returns depend on price movements in the
underlying commodity. The booklet describes them as high-risk and more
appropriate for informed investors.

Before using commodity derivatives, understand the commodity, market
drivers, contract terms and potential losses. The source suggests using
demo or mock-trading platforms where exchanges provide them before
committing real money.

29. DERIVATIVES: WHY THEY REQUIRE EXTRA CAUTION

Derivatives derive their value from an underlying asset. The
securities-market booklet describes futures and options as financial
contracts linked to underlying assets such as stocks or commodities and
classifies them as very high risk and potentially complex.

A derivative should not be approached simply because the initial amount
required appears smaller than the value of the underlying position.
Contract specifications, leverage, expiry, margin requirements,
volatility and settlement can produce outcomes that are difficult for an
inexperienced participant to manage.

The source explicitly advises understanding the underlying commodity,
market drivers and contract terms before investing or hedging.

A conservative guideline is: - Do not trade a derivative you cannot
explain. - Know the maximum possible loss under the strategy. -
Understand margin and settlement obligations. - Read the contract
specifications. - Do not rely on anonymous tips. - Use regulated
platforms and intermediaries. - Practice with a demo environment where
appropriate.

High complexity is itself a risk factor. The investor should be able to
explain not just how a trade can make money, but how and why it can lose
money.

30. INVESTMENT DISCIPLINE AND PORTFOLIO MONITORING

Investment discipline means following a process rather than reacting
emotionally to market movements.

The securities-market booklet recommends keeping copies of agreements,
application forms, statements and communications; regularly reviewing
account statements and portfolio performance; ensuring advice matches
the investor’s risk profile and objectives; and raising complaints
promptly when necessary.

A disciplined portfolio review asks: - Has the financial goal changed? -
Has the time horizon changed? - Has the investor’s income or liability
position changed? - Has the risk capacity changed? - Has the investment
thesis changed? - Are costs and taxes understood? - Are any holdings
excessively concentrated?

Do not confuse frequent monitoring with frequent trading. Reviewing a
portfolio can be useful; impulsive buying and selling based on daily
price movements can be harmful.

A contract note is an official legal record of a stock-exchange
transaction executed by a broker on behalf of an investor. The source
notes that it includes details such as quantity, price, brokerage, taxes
and total amount. Keep such records.

31. TAX TREATMENT OF SECURITIES-MARKET INVESTMENTS

The securities-market booklet explains capital gains as profit realized
when an investment is sold at a higher price than its purchase price.
The tax treatment presented depends on the holding period and type of
asset.

For the source’s stated framework: - Equity shares and equity-oriented
mutual funds held for 12 months or less are treated as short-term, with
a 20% tax rate plus cess as presented in the booklet. - Equity shares
and equity-oriented mutual funds held for more than 12 months are
treated as long-term, with 12.5% on gains exceeding Rs. 1.25 lakh per
financial year, plus cess as stated. - Physical gold and real estate
held for less than 24 months are presented as short-term and taxed at
the marginal slab rate. - Physical gold and real estate held for more
than 24 months are presented as long-term and taxed at 12.5% without
indexation under the source’s stated framework. - Debt mutual funds with
less than 35% equity exposure are presented as taxed as short-term
capital gains at the marginal slab rate irrespective of holding
period. - Equity-oriented hybrid funds with at least 65% equity exposure
are presented as taxed like equity mutual funds; other, debt-heavy
hybrid funds are presented as taxed like debt funds.

Tax rules can be amended. Therefore, these figures must be treated as
source-edition guidance and checked against the current law before
filing or transacting.

The key planning principle is to understand tax as one component of
return. A high nominal return can produce a different after-tax result
from a lower nominal return with a different tax treatment.

============================================================ PART III -
BANKING AND DIGITAL FINANCIAL SAFETY
============================================================

32. DEPOSIT ACCOUNTS AND GOOD BANKING PRACTICES

The FAME publication describes regular savings accounts as relatively
low-return “passive savings” and recommends considering recurring or
fixed deposits where a better return is appropriate for the purpose.

Good account-management practices include: - Take a passbook or account
statement and check entries periodically. - Keep cheque books, if
issued, in safe custody. - Never share internet-banking ID or
password. - Never share a debit-card PIN. - Never share OTPs received
for online transactions.

Nomination is important. A nominee is the person entitled to receive the
money in the bank account after the depositor’s death, in the capacity
described by the source. Nomination facilitates quicker claim settlement
and can reduce hardship for surviving family members.

The FAME publication advises filling the nomination form when opening a
deposit account, updating nomination details when necessary and
obtaining acknowledgment.

For term deposits, premature withdrawal may attract a penalty under the
applicable terms. The source also recommends opting for a
premature-withdrawal mandate for joint term deposits to facilitate claim
settlement.

The general banking rule is simple: maintain accurate records, protect
credentials, keep contact information updated and review account
activity.

33. INOPERATIVE ACCOUNTS AND UNCLAIMED DEPOSITS

FAME states that a savings or current account is treated as inoperative
when there are no customer-induced transactions for a period of more
than two years.

Segregating inoperative accounts helps reduce fraud risk and alerts bank
staff to the increased risk associated with such accounts.

The source states that a mandate for crediting interest on a fixed
deposit and/or dividend on shares is treated as a customer-induced
transaction.

For activation, banks have been advised to provide KYC-updation
facilities at all branches, including non-home branches, and through
Video-Customer Identification Process where the facility is available
and requested. The source states that banks have to activate the account
within three working days after complying with the applicable KYC
requirements.

The source also states that there are no charges for activation of an
inoperative account. Interest on savings accounts continues to be
credited regularly irrespective of whether the account is operative.

Banks are required, under the source’s stated framework, to display
lists of unclaimed deposits/inoperative accounts inactive for ten years
or more on their websites and provide search facilities.

This makes regular account monitoring important. Keeping accounts
active, maintaining updated KYC and ensuring that family members know
about relevant financial assets can reduce the chance that money becomes
difficult to trace.

34. PAYMENTS BANKS, SMALL FINANCE BANKS AND BUSINESS CORRESPONDENTS

The FAME publication explains that Payments Banks and Small Finance
Banks were created to promote financial inclusion through secure,
technology-driven services.

For Payments Banks, the source edition states that they can accept
demand deposits up to Rs. 2 lakh per customer, cannot accept
recurring/fixed deposits under the described framework, can issue
ATM/debit cards but not credit cards, and cannot provide loans and
advances. They may provide payment and remittance services and
distribute mutual-fund and insurance products.

Small Finance Banks are described as serving unserved and underserved
sections and providing small-ticket loans to small business units, micro
and small enterprises, small and marginal farmers and entities in the
unorganized sector. The source states that at least 50% of an SFB’s loan
portfolio has to constitute loans and advances up to Rs. 25 lakh under
the described framework.

A Business Correspondent (BC) is a bank representative or agent who
serves customers, often in remote areas. A BC can assist with opening
accounts, deposits and withdrawals, money transfers, collection of loan
applications and disbursal of small-value credit.

If using a BC, confirm the person’s identity and details with the bank.
The FAME source directs customers to approach the nearest branch for
information and mentions the BC Registry for locating BCs. Complaints
about BC services can be made to the bank.

35. RETAIL REMITTANCES

Retail remittance services allow individuals to transfer money through
formal channels. The FAME publication places retail remittances within
its digital financial-literacy section.

The key financial guideline is to use legitimate banking or regulated
payment channels, verify beneficiary details and retain transaction
records.

Before transferring funds, check: - Recipient name. - Account number or
identifier. - Bank or service provider. - Amount. - Purpose. -
Transaction reference.

For recurring family remittances, maintain a record of dates and
amounts. Formal channels provide transaction evidence and support
complaint or reconciliation processes when a problem occurs.

Never allow urgency or emotional pressure to replace verification.
Fraudsters often use emergencies, fake employment offers, fake
investment opportunities and impersonation to induce immediate
transfers.

36. UNIFIED PAYMENTS INTERFACE (UPI)

UPI is a platform for transferring money between bank accounts using a
smartphone with internet access. The FAME publication describes use
through BHIM, bank applications or third-party applications. It also
mentions UPI123Pay for feature-phone users through options such as
app-based functionality, missed call, IVR and proximity sound-based
payments.

To use UPI in the source’s described setup, the user needs a bank
account, a mobile number linked to the bank account and a compatible
device. A debit card linked to the account may be required for initial
setup, and UPI can also be linked to RuPay credit cards under the
applicable framework.

The source describes benefits including instant transfers, 24/7
availability and the use of virtual IDs to reduce the need to share
sensitive bank-account information.

UPI safety rules: DO: - Keep the UPI application updated. - Review every
collect request before accepting. - Recheck amount and beneficiary
before confirming.

DO NOT: - Share the UPI PIN. - Transfer money without verifying the
recipient.

Remember the fundamental security distinction: receiving money does not
normally require you to disclose your PIN. Treat any request for a PIN,
OTP or password as a major warning sign.

37. ELECTRONIC BANKING SAFETY

FAME recommends typing a bank’s URL using a verified and trusted browser
and using HTTPS-secured sites for payments. It advises using
difficult-to-guess passwords with alphanumeric and special characters,
changing passwords regularly and keeping payment applications updated.

Register the mobile number and email address with the bank and activate
transaction alerts. This makes unusual activity easier to detect.

Avoid: - Accessing the bank through unverified search results. - Storing
login credentials insecurely. - Entering credentials into untrusted
portals. - Using public computers or cyber cafes for sensitive
banking. - Transacting over unsecured or open public Wi-Fi. - Sharing
internet-banking passwords, mobile-banking PINs or OTPs with anyone,
including persons claiming to be bank staff.

The most important response to an unauthorized transaction is speed.
Notify the bank immediately. Keep the bank’s official contact details
readily available rather than searching for a number after a fraud has
already occurred.

38. ATM SAFETY

The FAME publication recommends using EMV chip-and-PIN debit cards
rather than magnetic-stripe cards and advises contacting the bank for
replacement where necessary.

At an ATM: - Inspect the area for suspicious cameras or skimming
devices. - Cover the keypad while entering the PIN. - Do not allow
another person to observe the PIN. - Count and check the cash
dispensed. - Collect the card before leaving. - Register the phone
number with the card-issuing bank for transaction alerts. - Block the
card immediately if it is lost, stolen or suspected to be compromised.

Never share card number, expiry date, CVV, PIN or OTP.

The source states that failed ATM transactions should be reported to the
card-issuing bank and that banks are required, under the stated
framework, to resolve failed ATM transactions within T+5 days or pay
compensation at Rs. 100 per day. The exact rules should be verified
against the current applicable framework when a dispute occurs.

39. FRAUDULENT DIGITAL TRANSACTIONS AND CUSTOMER LIABILITY

FAME strongly emphasizes immediate notification of fraudulent or
unauthorized electronic transactions. The longer the delay, the greater
the potential loss.

If fraudulent transactions continue after the bank has been informed,
the bank bears the loss under the conditions described by the source.
The bank should provide acknowledgment of the complaint, and the source
states that the bank must resolve the complaint within 90 days and
credit the amount involved within 10 working days from customer
notification under the described framework.

Customer liability depends on the circumstances. If the customer is
negligent, for example by sharing a password, PIN or OTP, the customer
bears the loss until the bank is informed.

Where there is no customer negligence and the customer reports
immediately, the source describes zero liability within the stated
three-working-day notification window. It also describes zero liability
in cases of bank negligence or deficiency and certain third-party
breaches where neither bank nor customer is at fault and the customer
reports within the stated period.

The source gives maximum-liability amounts for specified cases where the
system breach is elsewhere and notification is delayed four to seven
working days: - BSBD accounts: Rs. 5,000. - Certain other savings,
prepaid instruments, MSME and specified individual
current/cash-credit/overdraft accounts and credit cards within the
stated limits: Rs. 10,000. - Other current/cash-credit/overdraft
accounts and credit cards above the stated limit: Rs. 25,000.

These are source-edition figures and should be checked against the
applicable current rules.

40. ACCOUNT AGGREGATOR

The FAME publication describes the Account Aggregator (AA) framework as
a mechanism through which customers can securely share financial
information.

A key feature is consent. The customer instructs the Account Aggregator
to share financial information with Financial Information Users. The
source states that the Account Aggregator does not see or store the
customer’s financial information and that information is shared only
after clear customer instructions.

The framework can connect Financial Information Providers such as banks,
NBFCs, asset-management companies, depositories, insurance companies or
repositories and GSTN with regulated Financial Information Users.

Potential benefits include faster loan processing, easier credit
assessment, wealth management, personal-finance management and
reconciliation. The source highlights the potential for small borrowers
and MSMEs, including cash-flow-based lending.

The central safety principle is consent. Do not approve a data-sharing
request simply because it appears to be required for a service.
Understand what information is being shared, with whom and for what
purpose.

41. DIGITAL LENDING APPS

Digital Lending Apps are applications used to facilitate online loans.
The FAME publication explains that legitimate apps may be associated
with regulated banks or NBFCs or their authorized Lending Service
Providers.

Before using a digital lending app: 1. Check whether it is associated
with an RBI-regulated bank or NBFC. 2. Verify the relationship through
the bank or NBFC’s official website. 3. Avoid downloading apps sent
through random SMS or social-media links. 4. Read the Key Fact
Statement. 5. Understand the Annual Percentage Rate and total cost. 6.
Read the sanction letter and other loan documents. 7. Review privacy and
data-storage policies. 8. Examine app permissions before installation.
9. Confirm that documents are digitally signed and appropriately issued.

Suspicious loan apps should be reported to law-enforcement authorities
as stated by the source.

For grievances, contact the designated Grievance Redressal Officer of
the bank/NBFC or relevant LSP. The source states that if the complaint
is not satisfactorily resolved by the bank/NBFC within 30 days, the
customer can use the RBI Complaint Management System under the
applicable Ombudsman framework.

42. DIGITAL RUPEE

FAME describes the Digital Rupee as legal tender like physical currency.
A Digital Rupee wallet can be used to make payments to persons or
merchants having a Digital Rupee wallet.

The source states that Digital Rupee is interoperable with UPI, allowing
payment to merchants by scanning a UPI QR code. It also describes
loading and redeeming Digital Rupee through the linked bank account and
using the Digital Rupee application of a pilot bank under the framework
described in the source.

The important practical distinction is that digital payment technologies
can differ in architecture even when the user experience appears
similar. Users should use official applications, protect device
credentials and avoid accepting payment instructions from unknown
parties.

Never share a PIN or OTP to “receive” a payment. Verify the recipient
and amount before authorizing any transfer.

43. CURRENCY NOTES, COINS AND THE MANI APP

FAME notes that soiled, torn, damaged, mutilated or defective notes can
be exchanged at bank branches over the counter under the applicable RBI
Note Refund Rules referenced in the source. If a bank refuses an
eligible exchange, a complaint can first be lodged with the bank and, if
unresolved for the stated period, escalated through the applicable RBI
Ombudsman mechanism.

The source also explains that coins of different denominations can
remain in circulation simultaneously because coins have long lives and
that banks have been instructed to accept coins in transactions and
exchange them at branches.

The MANI App is described as an accessibility tool for visually impaired
persons. It can identify the denomination of specified Mahatma Gandhi
Series and Mahatma Gandhi New Series banknotes through audio in multiple
languages and vibration mode. The source notes that after downloading,
the app works offline and that it does not authenticate whether a note
is genuine or counterfeit.

44. UDGAM PORTAL

UDGAM stands for Unclaimed Deposits - Gateway to Access Information.
FAME explains that deposits remaining unclaimed for ten years are
transferred to the Depositor Education and Awareness Fund maintained by
RBI, subject to the applicable framework.

Banks are required to display unclaimed-deposit lists on their websites.
UDGAM was developed as a centralized search facility to help users
search for unclaimed deposits across participating banks.

The source states that users can register, search using the required
inputs and, after a successful match, obtain an Unclaimed Deposits
Reference Number (UDRN). The user can then approach the respective bank
branch to claim or reactivate the account following the bank’s
procedure.

This is particularly important when family members are unaware of old
accounts, deposits or investments. Maintain an inventory of financial
relationships and make sure legitimate family members can locate
necessary records.

============================================================ PART IV -
CONSUMER PROTECTION AND FRAUD PREVENTION
============================================================

45. MIS-SELLING

Mis-selling occurs when a financial product is sold in a manner that
does not match the customer’s request, needs or understanding, including
situations where important terms are not properly explained.

FAME uses an example of a customer opening a fixed deposit and being
pushed to purchase insurance. Its guidance is clear: do not
automatically combine savings with insurance. Assess insurance needs and
investment requirements separately.

Core rules: - Buy a product because you need it, not because an
intermediary pressures you. - Read the application form before
signing. - Never sign blank forms. - Ask for important terms and
conditions in writing. - If an institution sells an unrequested product
or fails to explain important terms, complain to the institution. - If
the response is unsatisfactory, use the appropriate grievance mechanism.

Mis-selling risk increases when the customer does not understand the
product. Financial literacy is therefore an important
consumer-protection tool.

46. TOO-GOOD-TO-BE-TRUE OFFERS

The FAME message “Too good to be True?” warns against fictitious lottery
prizes, free-money offers and requests for bank or card details.

A classic warning pattern is: “You have won money; send your bank
details to receive it.”

The source states that RBI and banks do not ask for bank-account,
credit-card or debit-card details, PINs or OTPs through unsolicited
email, SMS or phone calls in the manner described.

RBI also does not open personal savings/current/fixed-deposit accounts
for individuals or issue personal credit/debit cards in the manner
described by such scams.

A simple response to an unexpected financial offer is: PAUSE -> VERIFY
-> DO NOT SHARE -> REPORT IF SUSPICIOUS.

Do not let urgency, fear or greed override verification.

47. SACHET AND REGULATORY VERIFICATION

The FAME publication advises investing or depositing money only with
entities registered with or regulated by the relevant authorities,
including RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA or the Government, depending on the
product and activity.

The source warns against schemes promising extremely high returns and
against lenders who promise rates that appear implausibly low while
demanding processing fees.

Sachet is presented as a portal for reporting information or complaints
regarding entities that default on repayment of deposits or money
collected under schemes.

Regulatory verification should be performed before payment, not after a
loss. The existence of a website, logo, app or professional-looking
certificate is not proof of legitimacy.

For securities-market services, verify SEBI registration. For banking
and lending, verify the relationship with an RBI-regulated entity. For
insurance and pension products, verify the relevant regulated status.

48. CYBER FRAUDS AND DIGITAL HYGIENE

The securities-market booklet identifies several common cyber-fraud
mechanisms: Phishing: attempts to obtain sensitive information through
fake emails, messages or websites. Smishing: SMS-based phishing.
Vishing: voice-based impersonation to obtain financial information.
Malware: malicious software that can steal or lock data.

A broader investment-fraud ecosystem can include fake investment
advisers or apps, pump-and-dump schemes, social-media scams,
unauthorized entities, chit-fund or MLM-style frauds and fake
digital-asset platforms as described in the source.

Digital hygiene means reducing the attack surface: - Use official
websites and applications. - Keep devices and financial applications
updated. - Use strong, unique credentials. - Do not share OTPs, PINs or
passwords. - Avoid public or unsecured networks for sensitive banking. -
Verify URLs and domains. - Do not install financial apps from random
links. - Review transaction alerts. - Report suspicious activity
quickly.

The goal is not to become suspicious of every digital service. It is to
create a verification habit before authorizing a financial action.

49. PONZI AND PYRAMID SCHEMES

A Ponzi scheme promises high returns with little or no risk but pays
earlier participants using money from new participants rather than
sustainable investment profits. It collapses when the flow of new money
is insufficient.

The securities-market booklet identifies common warning signs: -
Unrealistic returns. - “Guaranteed” or extremely high monthly returns. -
Referral commissions for recruiting new participants. - Vague business
models. - Fake product names. - Pressure to invest immediately. -
Social-media or influencer-driven hype. - Unregistered operators.

A pyramid-like structure may depend heavily on recruiting new
participants. The fact that early participants appear to receive money
does not prove that the underlying activity is legitimate.

The correct response is verification. Check registration, understand the
business model, demand written information, avoid guaranteed-return
claims and do not invest based only on word of mouth or social-media
popularity.

If a suspicious scheme is encountered, report it to the appropriate
regulator, law-enforcement agency, cybercrime authority or complaint
platform as applicable.

50. INVESTOR RECORDS AND EVIDENCE

Financial records are evidence. Keep copies of: - Account-opening
documents. - Agreements. - Application forms. - Sanction letters. - Key
Fact Statements. - Contract notes. - Account statements. - Payment
receipts. - Email and SMS communications. - Complaint acknowledgments. -
Transaction references. - Screenshots of suspicious communications when
safe and appropriate.

The securities-market booklet specifically recommends keeping
agreements, application forms, account statements and communications. It
also emphasizes regular review.

When a dispute arises, a clear chronology can be decisive. Record the
date, time, amount, beneficiary, transaction ID, communication channel
and action taken.

Do not delete fraudulent messages before recording relevant evidence. Do
not continue communicating with a suspected scammer merely to collect
evidence if doing so creates additional risk.

51. GRIEVANCE REDRESSAL IN BANKING

The FAME publication defines grievance redressal as a system for
receiving and addressing customer complaints with emphasis on fair and
prompt resolution.

For complaints against covered RBI-regulated entities, the source
describes an alternate grievance framework involving the RBI Ombudsman,
Consumer Education and Protection Cells and the Consumer Education and
Protection Department.

The first step is normally to complain to the concerned regulated
entity. If the complaint is not redressed, no reply is received within
30 days, or the customer is dissatisfied with the reply, the customer
may approach the RBI Ombudsman where the complaint falls within the
applicable framework.

A complaint should contain the required details and evidence that the
regulated entity was approached. It should not already be pending or
settled before another forum in a manner that makes the complaint
ineligible under the scheme.

The source states that there is no charge for filing or resolving a
complaint under the RB-IOS framework and that customers need not pay
third-party agencies to file the complaint.

52. RBI COMPLAINT MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

The FAME publication describes the RBI Complaint Management System (CMS)
as a 24x7 online platform for complaints against RBI-regulated entities.

The source highlights online filing because it provides acknowledgment
and a complaint number and can support efficient handling.

A strong complaint should be factual rather than emotional. State: 1.
What happened. 2. When it happened. 3. Which account/product was
involved. 4. What amount was affected. 5. What was communicated to the
regulated entity. 6. What response was received. 7. What remedy is being
requested. 8. What evidence is attached.

Keep the complaint number and all subsequent correspondence.

53. SECURITIES-MARKET COMPLAINTS: SCORES

SCORES stands for SEBI Complaints Redress System. The securities-market
booklet describes it as an online platform designed to facilitate
investor complaints.

The source describes features including 24x7 online access,
acknowledgment with a unique complaint number, online tracking,
reminders, centralized storage and audit trails, and the use of
complaint trends by SEBI for policy and regulatory action.

Examples of complaints mentioned include: - Non-receipt of dividends or
bonus shares. - Delay in dematerialization. - Unauthorized trades by
brokers. - High charges or mis-selling by mutual funds. - Poor advisory
services by Investment Advisers.

The source’s process can be understood as: Investor lodges complaint ->
entity submits action taken report -> designated body may review -> SEBI
oversight/escalation occurs under the process.

The booklet states specific timelines for action and escalation. Because
procedures can be updated, users should check the current SCORES process
when filing.

The most important rule is not to delay. Prompt reporting preserves
evidence and improves the possibility of resolution.

54. ONLINE DISPUTE RESOLUTION

The securities-market booklet describes Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)
as a SEBI initiative for resolving disputes between investors and market
participants through digital conciliation and arbitration.

The stated benefits include an entirely digital process, time-bound
handling, accessibility and online status tracking.

The source describes a three-stage concept: - Online conciliation, where
a neutral mediator attempts to help parties reach agreement. - Online
arbitration if conciliation does not resolve the dispute. - A final
arbitral decision that is enforceable in law, subject to the applicable
framework.

The booklet also notes that ODR can be opted for where appropriate and
identifies a dedicated ODR platform.

Use ODR when the dispute falls within its scope and when the ordinary
complaint process has not resolved the issue. Keep all records and
understand the consequences of accepting a settlement or proceeding to
arbitration.

55. SAARTHI AND FINANCIAL-EDUCATION RESOURCES

The securities-market booklet describes SAARTHI as SEBI’s official
investor-education mobile application. The source says it provides
educational content on securities-market basics, mutual funds and ETFs,
investor rights, trading and settlement, financial calculators,
fraud-identification tools, updates and complaint-related resources.

The source lists calculators covering areas such as SIP, EMI, inflation,
goal planning and bond yield.

Financial education is itself a form of risk management. Before
investing in a complex product, use reliable educational material to
understand the product, then verify current regulatory requirements and
product documents.

The booklet also describes a SEBI Investor Awareness Test/Certification
Examination conducted with NISM and notes that the initiative is
intended to improve understanding of savings, investments and the
securities market.

56. A MONTHLY FINANCIAL ROUTINE

A robust financial system can be operated with a repeatable monthly
routine.

WEEK 1 - INCOME CHECK Record all income received or expected. Confirm
salary, business receipts, rent, interest and other sources.

WEEK 1 - OBLIGATION CHECK List EMIs, credit-card dues, utility bills,
school or education expenses and other mandatory payments.

WEEK 1 - SAVE FIRST Transfer the planned savings amount to the
appropriate savings or investment destination before discretionary
spending.

WEEK 2 - EXPENSE REVIEW Compare actual spending with the budget.
Identify categories that exceeded the plan.

WEEK 3 - SECURITY REVIEW Check transaction alerts and account
statements. Investigate any transaction that you do not recognize.

WEEK 4 - GOAL REVIEW Update progress toward major goals. Confirm whether
contributions remain adequate.

MONTH-END Update: - Cash balance. - Bank balance. - Emergency fund. -
Investments. - Outstanding debt. - Net worth. - Upcoming major expenses.

A financial routine works because it turns financial discipline into a
process rather than a motivation-dependent activity.

57. A GOAL-BASED INVESTMENT DECISION PROCESS

Use the following sequence before investing:

Step 1: DEFINE THE GOAL. Write the target amount and purpose.

Step 2: DEFINE THE TIME HORIZON. Classify the goal as short, medium or
long term according to the source framework.

Step 3: BUILD THE SAFETY NET. Ensure emergency reserves and necessary
protection are addressed.

Step 4: ASSESS RISK. Determine how much volatility and potential loss
can actually be tolerated.

Step 5: CHOOSE ASSET ALLOCATION. Decide the appropriate mix rather than
starting with a product name.

Step 6: SELECT THE PRODUCT. Compare safety, liquidity, return potential,
cost and tax treatment.

Step 7: VERIFY THE PROVIDER. Use regulated intermediaries and official
channels.

Step 8: DOCUMENT THE DECISION. Record why the product was selected and
what the intended holding period is.

Step 9: MONITOR. Review periodically rather than reacting to every daily
market movement.

Step 10: REASSESS WHEN CONDITIONS CHANGE. A change in goal, income,
debt, family responsibilities or time horizon may require a revised
plan.

58. A BORROWING DECISION PROCESS

Before taking a loan, ask:

1.  Is borrowing necessary?
2.  What is the purpose?
3.  Does the borrowing create value or support an important financial
    objective?
4.  Can the repayment be made on time from reliable income?
5.  What is the total cost?
6.  What is the annualized rate or Annual Percentage Rate where
    applicable?
7.  What fees and penalties exist?
8.  What happens if income falls?
9.  Is the lender regulated?
10. Have the Key Fact Statement and sanction documents been read?
11. Is an agent demanding a fee to “arrange” the loan?
12. Is the app or lender asking for excessive personal-data permissions?

Avoid borrowing to fund routine discretionary spending when repayment
capacity is uncertain. The FAME publication specifically warns against
irresponsible credit-card use to fund expenses.

For digital lending, verify the relationship between the app and the
regulated bank/NBFC before providing data or accepting a loan.

59. A FRAUD-RESPONSE PROCESS

If you suspect fraud, speed matters.

IMMEDIATE ACTIONS: 1. Stop further transactions. 2. Contact the bank or
financial institution through an official channel. 3. Block or secure
the affected card/account if necessary. 4. Change compromised
credentials using a trusted device. 5. Record the time and nature of the
incident. 6. Preserve relevant transaction IDs and communications. 7.
Obtain a complaint acknowledgment. 8. Report to the appropriate
regulator or cybercrime/law-enforcement authority where applicable. 9.
Follow the institution’s dispute process. 10. Escalate through the
appropriate grievance mechanism if unresolved.

Do not: - Send additional money to “unlock” a withdrawal. - Share
another OTP to reverse a transaction. - Install remote-access software
at an unknown caller’s request. - Trust a person merely because they
claim to be from a bank, RBI, SEBI or police. - Delete evidence. - Delay
reporting.

A legitimate-looking interface can be fake. The securities-market
booklet describes fake trading apps in which users see fabricated
profits and are later asked to deposit additional money before
withdrawal. A displayed profit is not proof that money exists.

60. A FINANCIAL-HEALTH CHECKLIST

Use this checklist at least periodically:

INCOME [ ] All income sources identified. [ ] Income timing understood.
[ ] Income is sufficient for current obligations.

EXPENSES [ ] Essential expenses identified. [ ] Discretionary expenses
tracked. [ ] Budget compared with actual spending. [ ] Unnecessary
expenditure controlled.

SAVINGS [ ] Save-first transfer made. [ ] Liquid savings available. [ ]
Emergency fund progressing toward target.

DEBT [ ] Every loan recorded. [ ] Interest and total repayment
understood. [ ] EMIs paid on time. [ ] Credit-card dues controlled. [ ]
Credit report reviewed.

INVESTMENTS [ ] Every investment linked to a goal. [ ] Risk understood.
[ ] Portfolio diversified. [ ] Provider/intermediary verified. [ ] Costs
and taxes understood. [ ] Statements reviewed.

PROTECTION [ ] Appropriate insurance needs reviewed. [ ] Retirement
planning underway. [ ] Nominees updated where relevant.

DIGITAL SECURITY [ ] Mobile number and email linked to bank. [ ] Alerts
active. [ ] Passwords strong and unique. [ ] PINs and OTPs private. [ ]
Financial apps updated. [ ] No credentials stored in unsafe locations.

CONSUMER PROTECTION [ ] Important documents retained. [ ] Complaint
channels known. [ ] Official regulatory resources bookmarked or
recorded.

61. COMMON FINANCIAL MISTAKES TO AVOID

Mistake 1: Saving whatever is left. Correction: save first and budget
the remainder.

Mistake 2: Keeping all money in cash. Correction: consider safety,
liquidity and the loss of purchasing power from inflation.

Mistake 3: Chasing the highest return. Correction: evaluate risk and
return together.

Mistake 4: Concentrating in one investment. Correction: diversify across
appropriate assets.

Mistake 5: Borrowing without reading the fine print. Correction:
annualize rates and understand total costs.

Mistake 6: Using credit cards irresponsibly. Correction: borrow only
within repayment capacity.

Mistake 7: Ignoring credit history. Correction: pay on time and review
credit information.

Mistake 8: Treating all market volatility as permanent loss. Correction:
distinguish normal volatility from fundamental investment risk while
still respecting the possibility of permanent loss.

Mistake 9: Buying insurance as an automatic investment add-on.
Correction: assess insurance and investment needs separately.

Mistake 10: Trusting “guaranteed” high returns. Correction: verify the
provider and reject unrealistic promises.

Mistake 11: Sharing OTPs or PINs. Correction: never share authentication
credentials.

Mistake 12: Downloading financial apps from random links. Correction:
verify through official institutional websites.

Mistake 13: Delaying fraud reporting. Correction: notify the institution
immediately.

Mistake 14: Failing to nominate. Correction: add and periodically update
nomination information.

Mistake 15: Leaving old accounts unattended. Correction: monitor
accounts and search for unclaimed deposits when appropriate.

Mistake 16: Investing without a goal. Correction: use a goal, horizon
and risk profile as the starting point.

Mistake 17: Taking social-media investment tips as professional advice.
Correction: verify registration and conduct independent due diligence.

Mistake 18: Trading complex derivatives without understanding them.
Correction: learn the contract, risks, margin and settlement before
participation.

Mistake 19: Ignoring taxes. Correction: evaluate after-tax outcomes and
retain transaction records.

Mistake 20: Believing a professional-looking app proves legitimacy.
Correction: verify the intermediary independently through official
sources.

62. INTEGRATED EXAMPLE

Consider an individual earning Rs. 60,000 per month.

The first step is not to choose a mutual fund or stock. The individual
should list expenses, liabilities and goals.

Suppose essential expenses are Rs. 30,000. A six-month emergency-fund
target under the source booklet’s guideline would be Rs. 1,80,000.

The person then identifies discretionary spending and establishes a
realistic savings target. If a 20% savings target is affordable, that
would be Rs. 12,000 per month. However, if a major education expense or
loan obligation exists, the allocation should be adapted to the actual
financial situation.

Next, the person identifies goals: - Short term: build the emergency
fund. - Medium term: purchase a vehicle. - Long term: retirement.

The short-term reserve should emphasize safety and liquidity. The
vehicle goal should use an instrument appropriate for its time horizon.
The retirement goal can potentially include long-term investments with
greater growth potential, provided the investor’s risk capacity allows
it.

Suppose the person considers borrowing for the vehicle. Before taking
the loan, the borrower compares the interest rate, Annual Percentage
Rate where applicable, processing fee, tenure, EMI, total repayment and
early-repayment conditions.

Suppose the person receives an unsolicited message promising “30%
guaranteed monthly stock-market returns.” The offer fails the
risk-return test and shows Ponzi/scam characteristics. The correct
response is to stop, verify registration and avoid sending money or
credentials.

Suppose an unauthorized UPI transaction appears. The person immediately
contacts the bank, preserves the transaction reference and obtains an
acknowledgment. The applicable customer-liability framework is then
determined based on whether the customer, bank or third party was
responsible and how quickly the transaction was reported.

This example demonstrates the central idea of the two publications:
financial well-being is a system. Budgeting, saving, borrowing,
investing, digital security and consumer protection cannot be treated as
unrelated subjects.

63. FINAL RULES

RULE 1 - KNOW YOUR NUMBERS. Know income, expenses, assets, liabilities,
savings and debt.

RULE 2 - BUDGET BEFORE SPENDING. A budget turns income into a plan.

RULE 3 - SAVE FIRST. Treat savings as a planned allocation.

RULE 4 - KEEP AN EMERGENCY RESERVE. Do not expose essential short-term
money to unnecessary market risk.

RULE 5 - MATCH PRODUCT TO PURPOSE. The right product depends on safety,
liquidity, return, goal and horizon.

RULE 6 - UNDERSTAND INFLATION. A rising balance does not necessarily
mean rising purchasing power.

RULE 7 - RESPECT COMPOUNDING. Time and reinvestment matter.

RULE 8 - BORROW RESPONSIBLY. Read the entire cost, not just the EMI.

RULE 9 - PROTECT YOUR CREDIT HISTORY. Repay on time and correct
inaccurate credit information.

RULE 10 - DIVERSIFY. Do not allow one investment or sector to dominate
without a deliberate reason.

RULE 11 - DO NOT CHASE RETURNS. Higher expected return usually means
greater risk.

RULE 12 - VERIFY REGISTRATION. Use regulated entities and official
channels.

RULE 13 - NEVER SHARE OTPs, PINs OR PASSWORDS. Treat requests for
credentials as a security warning.

RULE 14 - REPORT FRAUD IMMEDIATELY. Delay can increase financial loss
and reduce the ability to resolve the problem.

RULE 15 - DOCUMENT EVERYTHING. Statements, receipts, contract notes and
complaint numbers matter.

RULE 16 - SEPARATE INSURANCE FROM INVESTMENT DECISIONS. Buy protection
because it is needed and understand its terms.

RULE 17 - INVEST ACCORDING TO YOUR GOALS. A product’s popularity is not
a reason to own it.

RULE 18 - DO NOT BELIEVE GUARANTEED HIGH RETURNS. Risk-free high-return
claims are major warning signs.

RULE 19 - USE GRIEVANCE CHANNELS. First approach the regulated entity
where required; then escalate through the relevant mechanism.

RULE 20 - KEEP LEARNING. Financial literacy is an ongoing skill, not a
one-time qualification.
